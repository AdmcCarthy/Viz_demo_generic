Tuesday was the US midterms. On Wednesday markets rallied on relief that the uncertainty was over. By Thursday the mood had turned: investors read a Democratic sweep as a signal of tougher regulation on AI data centres, Qualcomm gave a weak forecast, and the most crowded trade of the decade, AI chips, came apart all at once.

Big point drops make big headlines. But a large index also falls by large numbers. The useful question is how much of the AI boom did those two days give back?

Wednesday 4 Nov

Relief rally

The day after the vote, the Nasdaq rose +1.44% to 25,736. The Dow added 1,569 points. The index was already about 7% below its October record, but the selling looked finished.

Thursday 5 Nov

The rout

The Nasdaq fell −5.52% to 24,315, down 1,421.5 points. That is the largest point drop ever recorded. Wednesday's gain was gone by lunchtime.

Micron and AMD each fell more than 17%. Nvidia fell 9%, losing roughly $450 billion of market value.

From the peak

Officially a correction

The index closed 11.9% below its 6 October record of 27,600. A fall of more than 10% from a high is the textbook definition of a correction.

Zoom out

Now look at the AI era

ChatGPT launched on 30 November 2022. The Nasdaq closed that day at 11,468. Over the next four years the AI trade carried it to 27,600, a rise of +141%.

The ledger

Two brackets

From ChatGPT day to the peak the index gained 16,132 points. From the peak to Thursday's close it lost 3,285.

The record-breaking Thursday on its own accounts for 8.8% of the boom's gains.

Friday 6 Nov, intraday

Still more than double

Even after the rout the Nasdaq is 2.1× its ChatGPT-day level. On Friday morning a strong jobs report and a 5.6% bounce in Nvidia lifted the index about 1.7%.

The dashed end of the line is an intraday reading, not a close.

So the worst point drop in Nasdaq history erased about one-eleventh of the AI boom's gains. That hurts, but it is a long way from a collapse.

Record-breaking points, ordinary percentages

Point records are mostly a sign of how high the index has climbed. In 1987 the whole Nasdaq was worth less than 450 points. Switch the ranking below and Thursday drops from first place into the middle of the pack.

The epicentre was the chips

The selling was concentrated. The memory and GPU makers whose order books depend on data-centre construction fell two to three times as far as the broad market.

How big is $2.5 trillion?

In one afternoon the Nasdaq lost more than the entire value of Norway's oil fund, which is three decades of saved petroleum revenue.

And the market value Nvidia lost in a single day is more than the whole company was worth on the day ChatGPT launched.

Friday: the bounce

Overnight the shock moved east. South Korea's Kospi, which is heavy in memory-chip stocks, opened down nearly 9%. Then US October payrolls came in at 165,000, far above the ~60,000 forecast, and dip-buyers came back to the chip stocks.

As of Friday morning the Nasdaq is still about 3.9% below Wednesday's close. A strong labour market also brings back an old worry, rate hikes, which could weigh on long-duration tech valuations.

So, is the bubble bursting?

Two days cannot answer that. What the numbers do show:

1. It was a record in points, not in pain. At −5.52%, Thursday ranks well below the worst days of 1987, 2000, 2008 and 2020, and below April 2025.

2. It was concentrated. Chip and AI-infrastructure stocks fell two to three times as hard as the index. The market was repricing one theme, the pace of the data-centre build-out, rather than the whole economy.

3. The boom is dented, not erased. About 80% of the gains since ChatGPT's launch are still in place, and the index is still more than double its November 2022 level.

This is a data story, not investment advice.

A detour

If 2026 were a hike

Analysts like to say the market "climbed a wall of worry". So we took that literally. We drew the Nasdaq's path from New Year's Eve to Thursday's close, then searched 20 real hiking and fell routes (1,100 km of track) for the stretch of ground with the same shape.

We cut every route into windows from 1.5 to 100 km long, walked each one in both directions, and scored it by correlation with the index. That came to 163,000 candidate hikes. Elevations come from a single global elevation model, so every trail is measured the same way.

The match works stage by stage. January's drift and the March sell-off are the descent from Clough Head into the col. The spring rally is the long pull up Great Dodd. The summer wobble is the saddle at Watson's Dodd. October's record is Stybarrow Dodd, and Thursday is the drop towards Sticks Pass.

The fit isn't perfect. On the fells the first summit is the higher one: Great Dodd beats Stybarrow Dodd by about 13 m. On the Nasdaq, October's record beat May's high.

Before you book the train to Keswick

A correlation of 0.96 sounds impressive, but we searched 163,000 windows to find it. To see what that much searching gives you by chance, we made 500 fake indices from random walks. Each was sampled on the same dates as the real Nasdaq and joined the same way, then given exactly the same search.

66% of the random walks found a closer trail match than the real Nasdaq did.

Meanwhile, in Jotunheimen

No open GPX file for Norway's classic hikes was within reach for this analysis, so we went one step further. We searched 128,000 straight-line transects across the 1°×1° elevation tile that covers central Jotunheimen, at every 10° of bearing and four lengths.

The best one scores r = 0.971, a better fit than any of the marked trails. It is a 6 km line in western Jotunheimen, about 18 km south-west of Galdhøpiggen. It is a straight line, not a path, so don't try to walk it.
